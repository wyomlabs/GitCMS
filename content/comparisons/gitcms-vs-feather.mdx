Feather has a clean pitch: write in Notion, publish a blog. Connect a Notion database, map your fields, pick a domain or a subfolder like `example.com/blog`, and you have an SEO-friendly blog with built-in analytics, email collection, and edge delivery. For teams that already live in Notion, it is one of the fastest paths from draft to published post.

GitCMS shares some of the same goals (a good writing experience for blogs, changelogs, and content sites) but approaches them differently. Instead of publishing from Notion, GitCMS gives you a **document-style visual editor** that writes directly to Markdown files in your Git repository. You own the content, you own the design, and you deploy on platforms like Vercel, Netlify, or Cloudflare.

Both tools are for teams that want to publish without heavy infrastructure. The question is whether your content should live in Notion or in your repo.

## What changed in 2026

Feather is a hosted platform for Notion-powered blogs. That made sense when building your own site was the expensive part. Now coding agents build a custom site, with marketing pages, docs, blog, and changelog on your own design, in a day, in a repository you own.

What that site still needs is **the admin**: an editor for the people who write, review before publish, and a safe way for AI agents to draft. GitCMS adds that to the site you already have, instead of moving one slice of your content onto a separate platform. That's Day Two: running the website after it ships.

## The writing experience question

Feather's writing experience *is* Notion. You write in Notion's editor, which is excellent, and Feather publishes it. If your team already uses Notion daily, there's no learning curve at all.

GitCMS gives you a **document-style visual editor** built for publishing: slash commands, drag-and-drop blocks, tables, callouts, and frontmatter as typed form fields. The patterns will feel familiar to Notion users, but the editor writes to Markdown and MDX files in your repo instead of a Notion database.

The editing experience is similar. The difference is where the content ends up.

## Notion dependency vs content ownership

This is the fundamental tradeoff.

### Feather: your content lives in Notion

With Feather, Notion is the source of truth. Your posts, drafts, and images live in Notion databases. Feather reads from Notion and publishes to your domain.

This means:

- **Your content is in Notion.** If you leave Notion, you migrate everything.
- **Publishing depends on two services.** Notion for content, Feather for publishing. If either has issues, your workflow breaks.
- **Content isn't in your repo.** You can't grep your posts, diff them, or ship them with the code that renders them.
- **History is Notion's.** Changes are tracked by Notion's page history, not Git commits.

### GitCMS: your content lives in your repo

With GitCMS, content is Markdown files in a Git repository. You own the files. They're portable, greppable, diffable, and versioned with every other file in your site.

You deploy the site yourself on Vercel, Netlify, Cloudflare, GitHub Pages, or any host. Content is part of the build, and deploys are atomic: content and code ship together.

That's more setup than Feather. You need a site (Astro, Next.js, or similar) and a deployment. But once it's set up, you own the content, the design, and the hosting.

## Own your marketing site

Your blog, changelog, and docs are part of your marketing site. They're often the first thing a potential customer sees, and the design, navigation, and the way content flows into the rest of the site all matter.

Feather gives you a blog that looks good, even on a subfolder of your domain. But it's Feather's templates, rendered by Feather, as a separate surface from the rest of your site.

With GitCMS, your content site is **yours**. Build it with Astro, Next.js, or any framework. Use your own design system. Your blog, changelog, docs, and marketing pages live in one codebase, share one design, and deploy as one site. That's the difference between "we have a blog on Feather" and "our site has a blog."

Coding agents like Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor make that site an afternoon project. Describe "a marketing site with a blog, changelog, and docs matching our product design," deploy it, and manage the content through GitCMS.

## Beyond blogs

Feather does blogs. If your content needs are broader (docs, changelog, marketing pages, menus and footers) you need additional tools.

GitCMS handles all of these in one admin:

- **Blog posts, changelog entries, and docs** as Markdown or MDX
- **Marketing pages** in MDX, with your components kept intact
- **Site data** like menus, footers, and team lists, edited as [forms](/docs/features/site-data/)
- **Multiple sites in one repository**, each configured separately

For teams whose content is just a blog, Feather's focus is fine. For teams that will grow beyond one, GitCMS grows with the site.

## Built-in analytics? Analytics is a solved problem

Feather includes built-in analytics: pageviews, visitors, referrers, and link clicks. That's convenient. But analytics isn't a CMS problem, and dedicated tools do it well:

- **Plausible**, **Fathom**, **Simple Analytics**: privacy-focused and lightweight
- **Umami**, **Matomo**: open source and self-hostable
- **Vercel Analytics**, **Cloudflare Web Analytics**: built into the host you already use
- **PostHog**: product analytics with funnels and session replays
- **Google Analytics**: free and comprehensive

One script tag gives you analytics as good as anything a CMS can bundle. Feather's built-in analytics are a convenience, not a reason to pick a content platform.

## AI agents and content workflows

Feather's AI story is Notion's. If you use Notion AI, you can draft and edit in Notion, and Feather publishes the result. The AI helps you write text, but it doesn't know your site, your collections, or your publishing process.

GitCMS's AI story is **MCP**. ChatGPT, Claude, and other MCP clients connect to GitCMS, read and edit entries and site data, draft inside content tasks, and submit for review. A person publishes. Coding agents work in the repo directly. GitCMS sells no AI credits; you bring the agent you already use.

The difference: Notion AI helps you write a post. GitCMS lets an agent propose a change to your website, with review before it goes live.

Feather's pricing is traffic-based. At low traffic, $39/mo for 10K pageviews is affordable. As the blog grows, so does the bill: $79/mo at 100K pageviews, $149/mo at 200K, $299/mo at 500K. Feather says it contacts you to upgrade rather than charging overages automatically, which is fair. But success still costs more.

GitCMS doesn't charge by traffic. Your content deploys with the site to your own host, where static pages are cheap to serve. See [GitCMS pricing](/#pricing) for the current plans.

## Where Feather is genuinely better

Feather is the stronger choice when **your team lives in Notion and wants to publish fast with zero setup**.

If your team:

- Already writes everything in Notion and doesn't want to change
- Wants a blog live in minutes, not hours
- Doesn't need to own the blog's design or hosting
- Wants analytics, SEO, and email collection without configuring anything
- Has a simple blog that doesn't need to live alongside docs or other content

Then Feather is genuinely convenient. The Notion-to-blog bridge works, and setup takes minutes.

## Where GitCMS is better

GitCMS is better when **you want to own the content, the design, and the workflow**.

Feather is a bridge between Notion and a blog. GitCMS is the admin for your whole website:

- **Own your content.** Markdown files in your repo, not Notion databases.
- **Own your design.** Build the site with any framework and match your brand exactly.
- **Own your hosting.** Deploy on Vercel, Netlify, Cloudflare, or anywhere.
- **Broader scope.** Blog, changelog, docs, marketing pages, and site data in one admin.
- **Review before publish.** Reviews in the editor, inline comments, and preview deployments (beta).
- **Agents through MCP.** AI assistants draft changes; people approve them.

## Decision by use case

**Notion-first team that wants a blog live in minutes:** Feather is the faster path.

**Team that wants to own the design, content, and hosting:** GitCMS is the better fit.

**Simple blog with built-in analytics and email collection:** Feather handles it out of the box.

**Blog, changelog, docs, and marketing in one repo:** GitCMS is the better fit.

**Traffic is growing and pageview-based pricing is a concern:** GitCMS with your own host is more predictable.

**Team wants AI agents drafting content with review before publish:** GitCMS is the better fit.
