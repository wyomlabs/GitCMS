Ghost is a focused, opinionated publishing platform. It does blogs, newsletters, and paid memberships well, and Ghost 6.0 added native analytics and distribution to the social web through ActivityPub, so readers on Bluesky, Mastodon, Threads, and Flipboard can follow a publication. If you are a creator, publication, or content team that wants a clean writing experience with built-in email and monetization, Ghost is a strong choice.

But Ghost is still a **Node.js application backed by a database**. It runs a server, stores its own content, and handles email delivery. That is the right architecture when publishing and monetization are tightly coupled.

For teams whose content is part of a broader site (docs alongside a blog, a changelog alongside marketing pages) GitCMS offers a simpler model: Markdown files in your repository, with one workflow for people and AI agents.

## What changed in 2026

Ghost is a hosted platform for blogs, newsletters, and memberships. That made sense when building your own site was the expensive part. Now coding agents build a custom site, with marketing pages, docs, blog, and changelog on your own design, in a day, in a repository you own.

What that site still needs is **the admin**: an editor for the people who write, review before publish, and a safe way for AI agents to draft. GitCMS adds that to the site you already have, instead of moving one slice of your content onto a separate platform. That's Day Two: running the website after it ships.

## Why this choice matters more than it looks

Ghost runs a Node.js server backed by MySQL. Whether you self-host or use Ghost(Pro), content lives in a database and is served dynamically:

- **Server dependency.** Self-hosting means running Node.js and MySQL, with uptime, updates, and backups on you.
- **Platform coupling.** Ghost bundles content, email, membership, and analytics into one system. That's convenient when you need all of it, and lock-in when you only need publishing.
- **Content in a database.** Posts are stored in Ghost's own Lexical JSON format, not Markdown. Exporting means Ghost's JSON export and a conversion step.
- **Standalone by design.** Ghost is a publication, not a site. Your docs, changelog, and product pages live somewhere else, usually on a different domain or behind a proxy.

With Markdown files in the repo, content is already on disk and deploys with the rest of the site. Docs, changelog, marketing pages, and blog posts all share one design and one workflow.

The split shows up in day-to-day work. A blog on Ghost usually means a second theme to keep in sync with the main site's design, a second set of logins, and a second place to look when someone asks "where do I change this?" When the blog is just another collection in the site's repository, there's one design system, one set of editors, and one place to look.

## Content as code

When content lives in Markdown files inside a Git repository:

- **`grep` works.** Search your entire content library with standard dev tools.
- **`git log` works.** Every content change has a commit, an author, a timestamp, and a diff.
- **`git blame` works.** Trace any sentence to the person or agent that wrote it.
- **Review works.** With review before publish, edits wait as a draft until someone approves them.
- **Deploys are atomic.** Content and code ship together. No database state to sync.

Ghost's writing experience is clean and focused, one of the best in any CMS, and its post history lets you restore earlier versions. But the content lives in Ghost's database, not in your repo, and it isn't portable without export tooling.

## AI agents and markdown

AI coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor) work natively with files in a repository. When your content is Markdown:

- Agents can **read, write, search, and diff** content with no API in between
- Content is **inspectable**: an agent opens the file and understands the structure
- Every agent edit lands in a Git commit, reviewable by people before it ships

Ghost has an Admin API, and you can connect agents to it with integrations. But an agent working through it writes straight into the live publication's database.

GitCMS has an **MCP app** for content agents. ChatGPT, Claude, and other MCP clients read and edit entries and site data, draft inside content tasks, and submit for review. A person publishes. GitCMS sells no AI credits; you bring the agent you already use.

## Where Ghost is genuinely better

Ghost is the stronger choice when your primary goal is **running a publication with email and memberships**.

If your team needs:

- A clean, distraction-free writing experience purpose-built for long-form content
- Built-in email newsletters with delivery, analytics, and subscriber management
- Paid memberships and subscriptions via Stripe
- Distribution to the social web without extra tools
- A standalone publication as the primary product
- A managed platform that handles hosting, email, and scaling

Then Ghost solves a specific problem very well. Its focus is its strength, and it's open source if you want to run it yourself.

## Where GitCMS is better

GitCMS is better when your blog is **part of a broader site, not the whole product**.

Most startup, product, and agency sites aren't standalone publications. They have:

- Docs and a blog on the same site
- A changelog alongside marketing pages
- Menus, footers, and team pages that also need editing (GitCMS edits these as [site data](/docs/features/site-data/) forms)
- Content that should ship with the code

GitCMS covers all of it in one admin: a visual editor, reviews in the editor, Google sign-in for invited editors, and AI agents that draft through the same review workflow. Ghost is designed for publications. GitCMS is designed for websites.

<Tradeoffs>
  <p>Choosing a code-first site with GitCMS over Ghost is a real tradeoff:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>Ghost's writing experience is one of the best, purpose-built for long-form publishing.</li>
    <li>Newsletters and email delivery are built into Ghost. With GitCMS, you need a separate email tool.</li>
    <li>Paid memberships and subscriptions are built into Ghost. GitCMS doesn't do memberships.</li>
    <li>Ghost(Pro) is fully managed. With GitCMS, you deploy the site on your own host.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>For teams whose content is part of a broader site and doesn't need newsletters or memberships, these tradeoffs are usually worth it. You get one workflow for all content, full portability, and a system that works for people and AI agents alike.</p>
</Tradeoffs>

## Decision by use case

**Standalone publication with newsletters and paid memberships:** Ghost is the better fit.

**Docs, blog, changelog, and marketing pages on one site:** GitCMS is the better fit.

**Creator who wants managed hosting, email delivery, and a social web presence:** Ghost is the better fit.

**Developer-led team that wants content as portable Markdown files:** GitCMS is the better fit.

**Blog as the primary product with subscriber management:** Ghost is the better fit.

**Startup or agency that wants AI agents and people working through one review workflow:** GitCMS is the better fit.
